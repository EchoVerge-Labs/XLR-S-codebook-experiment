Three null results on XLS-R: the acoustic codebook is not where low-resource ASR loses

Sinhala and Tamil are fit as well as English by XLS-R's frozen quantizer; pre-training list membership does not predict codebook fit across eighteen languages; and the penalty is not localised at any depth. A pre-registered programme of three experiments, reported with the confounds it cannot separate.

EchoVerge Labs · technical report · September 2026
Models: facebook/wav2vec2-xls-r-300m, facebook/wav2vec2-large-xlsr-53 (both frozen)
Data: ~15,500 clips · 24 layers · 2.7 M cached frames · 124 GB feature cache

Abstract

Self-supervised speech models such as wav2vec 2.0 [1] and its multilingual descendants XLSR-53 [2] and XLS-R [3] underperform on low-resource languages, and a common hypothesis attributes this to the frozen acoustic codebook: the discrete quantizer, learned mostly from high-resource speech, is assumed to lack entries for under-represented phonetic inventories. We test that hypothesis directly in three pre-registered experiments and fail to find support for it at any level.

Experiment A measures quantizer fit for Sinhala and Tamil against English on 4,999 clips with five deliberately corrupted positive controls. The residual usually assumed for this purpose is not computable in wav2vec 2.0, and the obvious substitute has almost no dynamic range — white noise scores only 1.11× English on it. Measured correctly, at masked positions, Sinhala and Tamil are fit better than English (InfoNCE 0.74× and 0.69×).

Experiment B scales this to eighteen languages in nine family-matched pairs split on XLSR-53 pre-training membership, evaluated on FLEURS [6]. The paired difference is statistically equivalent to zero under TOST [15] against a margin calibrated on Experiment A's controls, in three independent context regimes (p = 0.019 / 0.003 / 0.003).

Experiment C probes all 24 transformer layers with linear CTC [16] and speaker-identification heads at an exactly matched 3 h budget. Curve shape is uniform across languages against pre-registered criteria; speaker information peaks at layer 4 and phonetic content at layers 15–17 in every language, reproducing the expected dissociation [5]; and no language has saturated at 3 h.

One candidate positive signal emerged: CTC probes collapse far more often on Tamil and Sinhala than on English, concentrated at those languages' own best layers. A discriminating arm on Common Voice Tamil leans toward language over corpus but cannot settle it. We report the programme's instrumentation failures in full, including four that would have produced confident wrong answers.

1Introduction

The practical problem is Sinhala and Tamil automatic speech recognition. The proposed remedy, and the intended novelty of the wider project this report serves, was to extend XLS-R's discrete acoustic codebook with entries adapted to these languages. That proposal rests on an empirical premise: that the existing codebook represents Sinhala and Tamil speech measurably worse than it represents English. This report tests that premise before building on it.

The premise is intuitive. wav2vec 2.0 quantizes its convolutional output into a product codebook of two groups of 320 entries, learned by a Gumbel-Softmax relaxation [14] during pre-training. XLSR-53 was pre-trained on 56 k hours dominated by English audiobooks [2], and XLS-R on 436 k hours across 128 languages with a similarly skewed distribution [3]. If the codebook is a learned inventory of acoustic units, a language absent or scarce during pre-training might plausibly find no entries close to its sounds.

We find no evidence for this at three levels of analysis, and the negative results are bounded rather than merely non-significant. We also report, in full, the instrumentation defects encountered — a measurement with no dynamic range, a probe that returned exactly zero accuracy, optimiser divergence masquerading as depth structure, and a transcript-parsing bug that invalidated an entire arm — because in each case the naive reading would have been confident and wrong.

2Background

2.1 The quantizer

wav2vec 2.0 [1] encodes raw audio with a convolutional feature extractor producing latent vectors z, quantizes them into q via a product codebook, and trains a transformer with a contrastive objective: at masked time steps, the contextual representation must identify the true quantized target among distractors. Two properties matter here and are frequently misremembered.

First, the quantizer is not a reconstructing vector quantizer. Its codevectors are free parameters in their own space, selected by a linear projection of z, and are never asked to reconstruct z. In wav2vec2-xls-r-300m, z is 512-dimensional and q is 768-dimensional (two groups of 384). The classic VQ residual ‖z − q‖2 is therefore not defined: there is no shared space in which to subtract them. We verified both dimensionalities directly from the checkpoint.

Second, the contrastive alignment between the contextual representation and the quantized target holds only at masked positions. The objective never asks them to agree elsewhere. Section 4 shows that measuring without masking yields an instrument with essentially no dynamic range.

2.2 Pre-training language coverage

Establishing which languages each checkpoint saw turned out to require care. The XLSR paper [2] names only the experimental subsets used for its controlled comparisons — ten CommonVoice languages, ten BABEL languages, eight MLS languages — not the full 53. The authoritative enumeration is in the fairseq repository [17], which lists MLS [9] (8 languages), CommonVoice [8] (36) and BABEL (17) explicitly. De-duplicating by language yields 51 distinct ISO codes against the advertised 53; the discrepancy is in the published sources and does not affect any group assignment used here.

This matters for a specific reason: XLSR-53 pre-trained on Tamil but not on Sinhala. Tamil appears in its BABEL subset; Sinhala appears nowhere in the paper. XLS-R [3], by contrast, lists both among its 128 languages, drawn from VoxPopuli [10], MLS, CommonVoice, VoxLingua107 [11] and BABEL. We parsed XLS-R's Table 1 for per-language hours and validated the parse against the paper's stated total (436,154 h recovered against ~436 k reported).

3Common methodology

3.1 The measurement

All three experiments share one instrument, defined in Experiment A and imported verbatim thereafter. At masked positions — reproducing pre-training conditions with mask probability 0.065, span 10, 100 negatives, temperature 0.1 — we record the InfoNCE loss and the positive–negative cosine similarity gap between project_hid(c) and project_q(q). Experiment B imports these functions from Experiment A's module rather than reimplementing them; we verified that the imported probe reproduces Experiment A's stored per-file values to five decimal places.

3.2 Statistical approach

Three commitments recur. Pre-registration: decision criteria are fixed in a configuration file before any result exists, and amendments are recorded with the evidence that forced them rather than edited silently. Unit of analysis: frames within a file are autocorrelated and files within a language share a speaker pool, so aggregation runs frame → file → language and group tests use language means. Positive controls: every null is reported alongside a condition known to move the measurement, because a null from a dead instrument is worthless.

Absolute character error rate is never compared across languages in this report. Sinhala, Tamil and English differ in script, orthographic depth and character vocabulary (106 / 49 / 34 symbols), and at matched hours Sinhala supplies roughly half the character supervision over three times the classes. Only normalised curve shape is compared.

4Experiment A — quantizer fit

4.1 Data

4,999 clips: 1,000 each of Sinhala and Tamil in-the-wild YouTube audio (from a 200,137-clip, 990 h internal corpus), 1,000 each of read Sinhala (OpenSLR-52 [12]) and read Tamil (OpenSLR-65 [13]), and 1,000 Common Voice English [8]. Because the in-the-wild arm confounds language with recording condition, a domain-matched read-speech arm was added. 300 clips each of five positive controls were derived from the same English clips: white noise at matched level, time-reversed speech, 1.45× speed/pitch shift, six-way babble, and random sine chirps.

4.2 The instrument problem

Substituting the contrastive-space distance for the undefined VQ residual produces a metric that barely moves for anything. Across the full range from clean English to white noise it spans 1.11×. Any threshold-based criterion on it — including the ">1.5× indicates a gap" rule the study was originally specified with — is unreachable by any audio, and would return "no gap" regardless of the truth. Measuring at masked positions instead restores range: white noise collapses to a +0.010 positive–negative gap and an InfoNCE at chance (4.852 against ln 101 = 4.615), while speech sits near +0.45.

4.3 Result

Sinhala and Tamil are fit better than English on both masked metrics, in both acoustic domains. Controls applied: a domain-matched read-speech arm, source-matched codebook-usage statistics (perplexity grows with both frame count and speaker count), per-file energy gating so silence cannot drive the comparison, per-file significance testing, and a bf16-against-fp32 numerical check agreeing within 0.35% with 98.6% identical code assignments.

One adverse signal is recorded rather than suppressed: Sinhala and Tamil use a less diverse slice of the codebook (perplexity 0.91–0.95× English in the wild, 0.73–0.84× read). With an equal-or-better contrastive fit this reads as efficient coding rather than failure — white noise, which genuinely breaks the codebook, shows perplexity 0.02× and a collapsed gap — and every language including English uses only about 30% of the 320 entries per group.

Finally, running both checkpoints produced a natural experiment: on XLSR-53, where Sinhala was never seen and Tamil was, the two are statistically indistinguishable. Absence from pre-training produced no measurable codebook deficit. Experiment B was designed to test whether that generalises.

5Experiment B — membership across eighteen languages

5.1 Design

Nine family-matched pairs, fixed before any metric existed, split on XLSR-53 membership. Each language family contributes equally to both groups, so family cannot explain a group difference; several pairs are close relatives split across the membership line (Zulu/Xhosa, Estonian/Finnish, Tamil/Malayalam, Polish/Czech). FLEURS [6] is used because it derives from FLORES-101 [7], so the same source sentences are read across languages, removing the acoustic-domain confound Experiment A had to control separately. 500 test clips per language, English as normalisation reference.

The 1:1 family matching makes this a matched-pairs design, so the primary test is a Wilcoxon signed-rank on nine within-pair differences, with the minimum detectable effect reported alongside: dz ≥ 1.07 at 80% power, against 1.41 unpaired. Because tight pairing risks transfer from a seen relative masking a real effect, UNSEEN languages were additionally rated by whether a close relative was itself in pre-training.

5.2 Context control

Clip duration lowers InfoNCE — more context makes masked positions easier — and FLEURS languages differ in clip length by 68%, since the same sentence takes different times to say. Rather than choosing an analysis after seeing the balance, both arms were pre-registered: full clips, and a fixed-window crop. Two windows were run after the registered 25th-percentile rule proved to drop clips unevenly by language (Polish 47.6%, Persian 4.0%), which would have selected on the very confound being controlled.

5.3 Result

The effect is not merely non-significant but statistically equivalent to zero under TOST [15], in all three arms at both margins. Every 90% CI lies inside ±0.289. The claim this supports is bounded and positive: we can rule out a seen/unseen effect as large as the mildest acoustic corruption that measurably harms this codebook. All three arms split five pairs one way and four the other; the interleave is the result.

Three supporting analyses. The negative control behaved: applying the identical split to XLS-R 0.3B, where 18 of 19 languages were in pre-training and the split is meaningless, gives dz = −0.01, p = 1.000. Dose–response between XLS-R pre-training hours and fit is ρ = −0.37 (p = 0.135) over 33–69,493 h, but collapses to ρ = −0.014 (p = 0.966) once the six languages above 5,000 h are excluded — and those six are all European or English while all twelve below 321 h are not, so hours and region are almost perfectly confounded and the analysis cannot be interpreted either way. Variance decomposition using ω² and ε² rather than η² shows neither group membership nor family explains variance above chance (both negative).

Honest framing. With nine pairs this is "no significant difference at the dz ≥ 1.07 this design can detect", not "proven identical". The equivalence bound is what upgrades it, and its margin is borrowed from unpaired cross-corpus controls on a different checkpoint — a calibration anchor, not a formally matched equivalence margin.

6Experiment C — localisation by depth

6.1 Design

If the codebook is not the site of the penalty, where is it? Linear probes on each of the 24 transformer layers of wav2vec2-xls-r-300m, frozen, with all layers extracted once to a 124 GB fp16 cache (1,883,516 frames, zero failures) so probes never touch audio or the model.

Two tasks. Character CTC [16] as an ASR proxy, with a single linear layer — a deeper probe would measure the probe's capacity rather than the representation's. Speaker identification from mean-pooled features, which is script-free and therefore the only directly comparable cross-language number, and which doubles as instrument validation: the literature places speaker information early in the stack and phonetic content mid-stack [5]. A SUPERB-style learnable weighted sum over all layers [4] is run separately.

The two tasks require different splits and cannot share one: CTC uses a speaker-disjoint train/test split, while speaker identification necessarily shares speakers and splits utterances within speaker.

6.2 Instrument validation

Both required gates pass. A floor control — a randomly initialised model of identical architecture — produces no structure: CER 0.755–0.849 with a spread of 0.06–0.13 against real curves reaching 0.170, and speaker accuracy 0.10–0.27 against 0.88–0.96. And the expected profile reproduces three times independently: speaker identification peaks at layer 4 in every language while CTC peaks at layers 15–17.

Tamil's curve is 1.4× more peaked than English's, which would exceed a 25% threshold, but peakedness was not among the pre-registered criteria and played no part in the verdict; it is reported so that a measured quantity is not left unadjudicated.

6.4 The speaker-identification gap

Speaker accuracy is the only directly comparable cross-language number here, and there is a real gap: English 0.960, Tamil 0.914, Sinhala 0.878. A gap constant across depth points to task difficulty; a gap growing with depth points to representation. It does not grow — Spearman(layer, gap) = −0.042 (p = 0.85) and −0.001 (p = 1.00) — and is already present at layer 0. English is crowd-recorded across many devices while the OpenSLR corpora are studio-collected, so English speakers are separable by channel as much as by voice; Sinhala additionally has the shortest clips (4.29 s), giving less audio per utterance, which orders correctly with its larger gap.

The distinction this arm was built to make: a curve that shifts indicates data efficiency, one that plateaus below English indicates a representation ceiling. Nothing plateaus — all three are still descending at 3 h. At this budget these probes are data-limited rather than representation-limited. Whether the representation becomes limiting at larger budgets is untested, and the relative gains are not comparable across languages since each is measured against its own script-dependent baseline.

6.6 Probe stability as its own measurement

The CTC probe collapses into a degenerate solution on a minority of random initialisations. Our procedure detects these from training loss only and redraws, which is safe only if the amount of cleaning is even across the comparison. It is not. Measuring the rate directly — nine independent initialisations per cell, eight layers, three languages, 216 runs — gives:

By language, Sinhala and Tamil collapse 12/144 (8.3%) against English 0/72 — Fisher exact p = 0.010. By layer, within those two languages, L15/L17 collapse 8/36 (22.2%) against 4/108 elsewhere — p = 0.002.

The layer contrast is post-hoc. L15 and L17 were identified as best layers from the CER curves, and the collapse test then asked whether collapse concentrates there; the p-value is uncorrected for that selection. The language contrast was the question the measurement set out to answer and stands as reported.

Crucially, the cleaning does not distort the reported curves. Surviving-run CER over nine draws differs from the three-seed sweep by ≤ 0.003 at L15 and L17 for every language, so the UNIFORM verdict on shape holds. The instability is a separate finding about how reliably a probe can be fitted, not about where information sits.

6.7 An unexplained mechanism

CTC collapse normally occurs where a probe cannot find an alignment — at poor layers. Here it occurs at the best layers. Two candidate readings, neither testable with this design: a sharper optimum may sit in a more rugged basin, so initialisation matters most where the achievable fit is best; or poor layers may converge trivially to a bad-but-stable solution and never diverge, leaving informative layers as the only place non-trivial optimisation, and therefore failure, can happen.

6.8 Language or corpus?

"Sinhala and Tamil collapse, English does not" is observationally identical to "OpenSLR collapses, Common Voice does not". A Common Voice Tamil arm — same language as one pole, same corpus as the other, built to the same 3.00 h / 0.50 h budget with identical preprocessing and hyperparameters — was run to break the tie.

Pairwise it remains unresolved: CV Tamil collapses 4/72 (5.6%), indistinguishable from OpenSLR Tamil (p = 0.53) and from CV English (p = 0.12). Two things lean toward language rather than corpus: pooled, the language contrast reaches p = 0.017 while the corpus contrast does not (p = 0.069); and CV Tamil reproduces the mid-stack concentration both OpenSLR arms show (11.1% at L15/L17) which CV English never shows at any layer. Neither pooled contrast was pre-registered and they are not independent, so they are suggestive only. CV Tamil's train split also has only 22 speakers against 40, and Sinhala has no second corpus, so its arm remains fully confounded.

7Discussion

Three experiments at three levels of analysis fail to locate a low-resource representational deficit in XLS-R. The quantizer fits Sinhala and Tamil at least as well as English; membership in the pre-training language list does not predict fit across eighteen family-matched languages, to within an equivalence bound calibrated on known-harmful corruption; and the depth profile of usable information is the same for all three languages tested, with no sign of a representation ceiling at a 3 h labelled budget.

The implication for the originating proposal is direct: extending the acoustic codebook is not supported by any of this evidence and should not be the core novelty. A codebook that already represents these languages as well as it represents English has no headroom to recover by adding entries.

The one candidate signal is probe instability. It is genuinely localised in both language and depth, it is the only such effect found anywhere in the programme, and it is unexplained. We report it as a candidate rather than a finding because its layer component is post-hoc and its language-versus-corpus attribution could not be settled.

A broader methodological observation: in this programme the instrumentation was harder than the science. Six defects were found and fixed, and in every case the naive reading of the broken instrument was confident and wrong — a metric with 1.11× total range, a probe returning exactly zero, optimiser divergence that manufactured depth structure, a blind spot in the fix for that divergence, stale hyperparameters leaving learned weights at their uniform initialisation, and a transcript-parsing bug that made an entire arm look like a probe failure. Appendix B documents each.

8Limitations

  • Read speech throughout. Every experiment except Experiment A's YouTube arm is read-speech-anchored, while the target application is scraped in-the-wild audio. This is a scope limit of the whole evidential base.
  • Not proof of identity. Experiment B establishes "no difference larger than ±0.289", not "no difference". The margin is borrowed from unpaired cross-corpus controls on a different checkpoint.
  • Small n. Nine pairs in B; three languages in C. Subgroup splits (n = 5 and n = 4) are underpowered by construction and reported descriptively. No inferential claim about languages in general.
  • 3 h is a small probe budget. It compares curve shape across depth; it cannot establish achievable CER, and only the scaling arm separates a data limit from a representation limit.
  • CER is script-dependent and is never compared across languages. Matching on hours does not equalise character supervision.
  • Language and corpus are confounded throughout. One arm was built to break this and returned unresolved; speaker accuracy and absolute CER carry the confound untested.
  • Cleaning was not even. Collapse rate varies by language and layer. Surviving-run estimates are unaffected (≤ 0.003), but the discarding was not uniform across the comparison.
  • Still open: a penalty that is diffuse rather than localised; one that emerges only above 3 h; or one specific to in-the-wild audio.

9Conclusion

The acoustic codebook is not where Sinhala and Tamil lose. Neither is membership in the pre-training language list, nor any particular depth of the network. At a 3 h labelled budget these probes are data-limited rather than representation-limited, and equally so for English. What remains is a candidate signal in probe stability rather than probe accuracy — a localised, unexplained, and not-yet-attributable effect that would need its own study.

AAppendix: reproducibility

All code, pre-registered configurations, decision criteria and results are at github.com/EchoVerge-Labs/XLR-S-codebook-experiment. Audio, extracted features and staged copies are not versioned; every script regenerates what it needs.

Environment

NVIDIA DGX Spark (GB10, ARM64, compute capability 12.1, CUDA 13.0, driver 580.82.09), PyTorch 2.14.0+cu130, transformers 5.16.1, Python 3.12. ARM64 note: the commonly cited cu121 wheels do not exist for aarch64 and would not support sm_121 if they did; the cu130 index is required.

Key protocol constants

Masked probe: mask probability 0.065, span 10, 100 negatives, temperature 0.1, 3 mask draws per file, seeds 1000·file_id + k, bf16, 30 s clip cap. CTC probe: single linear layer, lr 1e-2, batch 32, 25 epochs, AdamW, weight decay 0.01, gradient clip 5.0, linear warmup 10%, 3 seeds, raw (unstandardised) features. Speaker probe: mean-pooled, lr 1e-3, batch 64, 30 epochs, 3 training utterances per speaker. Energy gate: frames with RMS above 10% of the file's 90th percentile.

BAppendix: errata and instrument defects

Each of the following was found during the work, is documented in the repository with the evidence that forced the change, and would have produced a confident wrong answer if missed.

  1. Undefined residual. ‖z − q‖2 is not computable in wav2vec 2.0 (512-d vs 768-d, non-reconstructing quantizer). Replaced with masked contrastive metrics.
  2. Dead metric. The unmasked contrastive distance spans 1.11× from clean English to white noise, making any threshold criterion on it unreachable. Superseded by the masked measurement.
  3. Speaker probe returning exactly 0.0000. The speaker split spans both CTC caches; looking up one cache trained on 30 speakers and tested on a disjoint 10. Fixed to span both.
  4. Ceiling effect. The corrected speaker probe then sat at 0.93–1.00 on every layer, unable to resolve depth. Subsampled to 3 training utterances per speaker.
  5. Optimiser divergence as fake structure. Roughly one CTC seed in three collapsed on some layers (Sinhala L15: 0.837 / 0.274 / 0.270), which averages into spurious depth structure. Gradient clipping, a negative blank-bias initialisation and per-layer feature standardisation all failed; a within-cell training-loss outlier rule with redraws worked (SD 0.267 → 0.005).
  6. Blind spot in that fix. The rule needs a healthy sibling, so a cell where every seed collapses looks self-consistent. Tamil's 0.5 h scaling point reported 0.7790 with nothing flagged; nine fresh draws found 4 healthy at 0.216 and 5 collapsed at 0.774. The procedure now always draws three more initialisations than it keeps.
  7. Stale hyperparameters. The weighted-sum speaker probe reused settings that could not learn 24 layer weights; they stayed at the uniform initialisation (centroid 11.50, spread 1.04×) and every score fell below that language's best single layer.
  8. TSV quoting bug. csv.DictReader with a tab delimiter applies quote rules by default, so any sentence containing a quote character swallowed subsequent tabs and concatenated later columns into the transcript. Test references averaged 150 characters on 4.6 s clips. QUOTE_NONE recovered 298 rows and eliminated all 19 corrupted sentences (longest 18,309 characters). This invalidated the first Common Voice Tamil arm, whose best CER of 0.778 equalled the random-init floor; its numbers are withdrawn. The three main arms were verified clean.
  9. Withdrawn variance claim. An earlier draft reported that family explains "28× more variance than membership" (η² 0.246 vs 0.009). η² rewards degrees of freedom; against the null expectation both factors fall below chance (ω² −0.050 and −0.155).
